# Knowledge notes

Curation summaries of published, dated positions; not an independent scientific review. Reuse of the style sources as knowledge is declared in the JSON.

## K01-elementary-mates

In mates against a lone king, the principle is to drive the opposing king to the last line with the help of your own king, without falling into stalemate. With two bishops the king must go to a corner; with bishop and knight, only to a corner of the bishop's colour.

[Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_37278f95c5a04857); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_daa42b12f5ca40a6)

- "The principle is to drive the opposing King to the last line on any side of the board." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "In all endings of this kind, care must be taken not to drift into a stale mate." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "In this particular ending one should remember that the King must not only be driven to the edge of the board, but also into a corner." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "With a Knight and a Bishop the mate can only be given in the corners of the same colour as the Bishop." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play; first published in 1921)

Limits: Rules from Chess Fundamentals (1921), confirmed by endgame theory. The book also gives move-count targets for practice; they are the author's benchmarks, not norms.

## K02-king-and-pawn-and-opposition

In king and pawn against king, he says to advance the king as far as the pawn's safety allows and to move the pawn only when its safety requires. In his wording, with the kings on the same line and an even number of squares between them, the side to move has the opposition; against a waiting move, advance the king leaving one line free between the kings.

[Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_37278f95c5a04857); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_daa42b12f5ca40a6)

- "advance the King as far as is compatible with the safety of the Pawn and never to advance the Pawn until it is essential to its own safety." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "when the Kings are on the same line and the number of intervening squares between them is even, the player who has the move has the opposition." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play; first published in 1921)
- "Therefore it is seen that in such cases when the opponent makes a so-called waiting move, you must advance, leaving a rank or file free between the Kings." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play; first published in 1921)

Limits: The equivalent modern wording: with an odd number of squares between the kings, the side not to move has the opposition. Rook-pawn cases and exceptions in pawn endings are not covered here.

## K03-pawn-endings

In pawn endings, advance first the pawn with no opposing pawn in front of it, and act at once on the wing where you have more pawns. Before deciding, count whether the opposing king can catch the passed pawn and which pawn queens first.

[Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_37278f95c5a04857); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_daa42b12f5ca40a6)

- "It is generally advisable to advance the Pawn that is free from opposition." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "In these cases the general rule is to act immediately on the side where you have the superior forces." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "The first thing is to find out, by counting, whether the opposing King can be in time to stop the passed Pawn from Queening." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play; first published in 1921)

Limits: General principles from Chess Fundamentals; concrete positions need calculation.

## K04-bishop-and-pawns

With a bishop, he recommends keeping your own pawns on squares of the opposite colour to the bishop. The bishop's greatest weakness: with a rook's pawn that queens on a square of the other colour and the opposing king in front of the pawn, the bishop cannot win.

[Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_daa42b12f5ca40a6)

- "Whenever you have a Bishop, whether the opponent has also one or not, keep your Pawns on squares of the opposite colour to that of your own Bishop." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play; first published in 1921)
- "It is the greatest weakness of the Bishop, that when the Rook's Pawn Queens on a square of opposite colour and the opposing King is in front of the Pawn, the Bishop is absolutely worthless." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter II: Further principles in end-game play; first published in 1921)

Limits: A general rule with exceptions: in the ending, pawns on the bishop's colour can be protected by it. The rook's-pawn case is established endgame theory.

## K05-bishop-knight-and-bishop-pair

For theoretical purposes bishop and knight are worth the same, but in his opinion the bishop is usually more useful, and two bishops are almost always better than two knights. With pawns on both wings the bishop's range counts; in a blocked position the knight can be better.

[My Chess Career, chapter 8](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_8) (first published in 1920; doc_0ef790774798407c); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_37278f95c5a04857); [Chess Fundamentals, game 14: Queen'S Gambit Declined (F. J. Marshall v. J. R. Capablanca)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_3f09b29b2d3844eb)

- "and it is well known that two Bishops are almost always better than two Knights." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "with Pawns on both sides of the board the Bishop is superior because of its long range." (Chess Fundamentals, game 14: Queen'S Gambit Declined (F. J. Marshall v. J. R. Capablanca); first published in 1921)
- "he thinks that the Kt will probably be stronger in this position, which is somewhat blocked, to use the technical term." (My Chess Career, chapter 8; first published in 1920)

Limits: A 1921 opinion that later theory broadly confirms, but the assessment depends on the pawn structure. The books' table of piece equivalences was left out as dated and position-dependent.

## K06-opening-principles

His opening principles: develop quickly; before development is complete, do not move the same piece twice, except to win material or gain freedom of action; move pieces rather than pawns; bring out the knights before the bishops.

[Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_37278f95c5a04857); [My Chess Career, Conclusion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Conclusion) (first published in 1920; doc_58ad5fec36d64d41); [Chess Fundamentals, game 2: Queen'S Gambit Declined (A. K. Rubinstein v. J. R. Capablanca)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_6eefe003abc34668)

- "Before development has been completed no piece should be moved more than once, unless it is essential in order to obtain either material advantage or to secure freedom of action." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "In the openings, whenever possible, pieces should be moved in preference to Pawns." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter I: First principles: endings, middle-game and openings; first published in 1921)
- "except that the Knights ought to come out before the Bishops." (Chess Fundamentals, game 2: Queen'S Gambit Declined (A. K. Rubinstein v. J. R. Capablanca); first published in 1921)
- "You might say, with regard to the openings, that the main principle is rapid and efficient development." (My Chess Career, Conclusion; first published in 1920)

Limits: "Knights before bishops" is a 1921 rule of thumb with many exceptions in current theory. The opening examples in the books use lines from 1909 to 1921; concrete theory has changed.

## K07-unknown-opening-move

In the lecture that closes My Chess Career, his advice to a beginner facing an unknown opening move is to play the common-sense move: bring the pieces out quickly and put them somewhere safe, accepting it may not be the best move and taking a lesson from it for the next game.

[My Chess Career, Conclusion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Conclusion) (first published in 1920; doc_58ad5fec36d64d41)

- "Play what you might call the common-sense move." (My Chess Career, Conclusion; first published in 1920)
- "Bring your pieces out quickly, and put them in a safe place. You may not make the best move by doing this, but it will be a lesson for the next game." (My Chess Career, Conclusion; first published in 1920)

Limits: Advice for beginners; advanced players need to study theory.

## K08-centre

For him, control of the centre is an essential condition for a successful attack on the king. Against a demonstration on the king's side, regrouping the pieces and advancing in the centre neutralises the attack.

[My Chess Career, chapter 10](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_10) (first published in 1920; doc_23535ae83aa34787); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_d16b7617388b4ad9)

- "control of the centre was an essential condition to a successful attack against the King" (Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games; first published in 1921)
- "White needs only to rearrange his pieces a little and then advance against Black's centre, which will automatically stop any demonstration on the King's side." (My Chess Career, chapter 10; first published in 1920)

Limits: He even says no violent attack succeeds without controlling at least two of the four central squares; take it as guidance, not law.

## K09-threats-and-two-wings

Threatening is always an advantage, but a threat should be carried out only if something is gained at once. A typical plan is to make a demonstration on one wing to draw the opponent's pieces there and then, with greater mobility, switch the forces quickly to the other wing.

[My Chess Career, chapter 8](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_8) (first published in 1920; doc_0ef790774798407c); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter IV: General theory](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_39986d8149814e1d)

- "It is always an advantage to threaten something, but such threats must be carried into effect only if something is to be gained immediately." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter IV: General theory; first published in 1921)
- "make a demonstration on one side, so as to draw the forces of your opponent to that side, then through the greater mobility of your pieces to shift your forces quickly to the other side" (Chess Fundamentals, chapter IV: General theory; first published in 1921)
- "fix as many White pieces as possible on the Q's side by threatening P — Q Kt 5, then somewhat to break up the K's side through P — K Kt 4" (My Chess Career, chapter 8; first published in 1920)

Limits: General middle-game principles; they depend on the relative mobility of the pieces.

## K10-pieces-out-of-play

Leaving an opposing piece out of action is, in practice, like having an extra piece. It is also worth exchanging one of your own pieces that does little for the opposing piece that holds the defence together.

[Chess Fundamentals, chapter IV: General theory](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_39986d8149814e1d); [My Chess Career, chapter 7](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_7) (first published in 1920; doc_9eaf7c41def94386)

- "In such cases we might say that from that moment the game is won, because for all practical purposes there will be one more piece on one side than on the other." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter IV: General theory; first published in 1921)
- "The fact is that the B is doing very little, while the Black Kt at Q 4 is the key to Black's defence, hence the necessity of exchanging the almost useless B for a most valuable Kt." (My Chess Career, chapter 7; first published in 1920)

Limits: Examples from games of 1913 to 1919; the idea is general.

## K11-pawn-structure

Pawns side by side on the same rank are strongest; an isolated pawn is weak and a target, and having one without compensation should be avoided. A passed pawn grows stronger as pieces leave the board, and doubled pawns can exert great pressure.

[Chess Fundamentals, game 14: Queen'S Gambit Declined (F. J. Marshall v. J. R. Capablanca)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_3f09b29b2d3844eb); [My Chess Career, chapter 5](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_5) (first published in 1920; doc_cf728526e8ce4e81); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_d16b7617388b4ad9); [Chess Fundamentals, game 9: Queen'S Gambit Declined (J. R. Capablanca v. R. Teichmann)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_d49fe07489a74dda)

- "In an abstract way we may say that two or more Pawns are strongest when they are in the same rank next to one another." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games; first published in 1921)
- "The drawback to the text move is that it leaves Black's Q P isolated, and consequently weak and subject to attack." (Chess Fundamentals, game 9: Queen'S Gambit Declined (J. R. Capablanca v. R. Teichmann); first published in 1921)
- "Black has now an isolated P and no compensation for it, a thing that should always be avoided." (My Chess Career, chapter 5; first published in 1920)
- "it might be generally said that a passed Pawn increases in strength as the number of pieces on the board diminishes" (Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games; first published in 1921)
- "Though doubled and isolated this Pawn exercises enormous pressure." (Chess Fundamentals, game 14: Queen'S Gambit Declined (F. J. Marshall v. J. R. Capablanca); first published in 1921)

Limits: General assessments; an isolated pawn can be worth the initiative it gives, as he himself shows in My Chess Career.

## K12-rook-endings

Rook-and-pawn endings are the most common and few players master them. With the defending king cut off by two files and the pawn able to reach the fourth rank, a win is generally possible; the winning technique often ties the opponent's rooks to the defence of pawns and keeps your own free.

[Chess Fundamentals, game 14: Queen'S Gambit Declined (F. J. Marshall v. J. R. Capablanca)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_3f09b29b2d3844eb); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter V: End-game strategy](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_b4c6a546a7d74e5d)

- "there are two files between the opposing King and the Pawn from which the King is cut off by the Rook, and besides, the Pawn can advance to the fourth rank" (Chess Fundamentals, chapter V: End-game strategy; first published in 1921)
- "The winning tactics in all these endings have merely consisted in keeping the opponent's Rooks tied to the defence of one or more Pawns, leaving my own Rooks free for action." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter V: End-game strategy; first published in 1921)
- "The powerful position of the Black Rook at Q 7 fully compensates Black for the Pawn minus." (Chess Fundamentals, game 14: Queen'S Gambit Declined (F. J. Marshall v. J. R. Capablanca); first published in 1921)

Limits: Rules of thumb from 1921; modern rook-ending theory is more detailed. An active rook on the seventh rank can make up for a pawn, as in one of the book's games.

## K13-king-in-the-ending

In the ending the king should go to the centre of the board; with queens and many pieces on, bringing the king out usually helps the opponent. In one of the book's games, though, the king becomes a fighting piece even with queens on the board.

[My Chess Career, chapter 8](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_8) (first published in 1920; doc_0ef790774798407c); [Chess Fundamentals, game 12: French Defence (J. R. Capablanca v. O. Chajes)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_6f296454014d460a); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter V: End-game strategy](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_b4c6a546a7d74e5d)

- "The move is justified at least on the general rule that in such endings the King should be in the middle of the board." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter V: End-game strategy; first published in 1921)
- "He only helps White by coming out with the K." (My Chess Career, chapter 8; first published in 1920)
- "It is an instance of the King becoming a fighting piece, even while the Queens are still on the board." (Chess Fundamentals, game 12: French Defence (J. R. Capablanca v. O. Chajes); first published in 1921)

Limits: A general rule; when to activate the king depends on its safety in the concrete position.

## K14-opposite-coloured-bishops

Opposite-coloured bishops are the worse side's best drawing chance, but they do not guarantee a draw: with other advantages, the ending can be easy to win.

[Chess Fundamentals, game 9: Queen'S Gambit Declined (J. R. Capablanca v. R. Teichmann)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_d49fe07489a74dda)

- "Black exchanges Knights to remain with Bishops of opposite colours, which gives him the best chance to draw." (Chess Fundamentals, game 9: Queen'S Gambit Declined (J. R. Capablanca v. R. Teichmann); first published in 1921)
- "There are now Bishops of opposite colour, but nevertheless White has an easily-won game." (Chess Fundamentals, game 9: Queen'S Gambit Declined (J. R. Capablanca v. R. Teichmann); first published in 1921)

Limits: The assessment depends on the pawn structure and the other pieces.

## K15-defence

A cramped side should exchange pieces, because with fewer pieces there are more chances to escape. Defend the king with as few pieces as possible and bring them all forward only to attack the opposing king; if losing a pawn is unavoidable, give it up where it weakens the position least.

[My Chess Career, Conclusion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Conclusion) (first published in 1920; doc_58ad5fec36d64d41); [Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_d16b7617388b4ad9); [Chess Fundamentals, game 7: Ruy Lopez (J. R. Capablanca v. A. Burn)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33870) (first published in 1921; doc_df84a4044d194641)

- "In such cases, the less the number of pieces on the board, the better chances there are to escape." (Chess Fundamentals, chapter VI: Further openings and middle-games; first published in 1921)
- "You must always try and defend your King with as few pieces as you can, and it is only when attacking your opponent's King that you must bring forward all the pieces you can." (My Chess Career, Conclusion; first published in 1920)
- "Since he had no way to prevent the loss of a Pawn, he should have given it up where it is, and played Q Kt - Q 2, in order to make his position more solid." (Chess Fundamentals, game 7: Ruy Lopez (J. R. Capablanca v. A. Burn); first published in 1921)

Limits: General advice; active defence (R09) is usually better than passive defence.

## K16-advice-from-the-1919-lecture

The conclusion of My Chess Career reproduces a lecture of his from 1919. There he asks for the courage of one's convictions (if you think your move is good, make it) and, when attacking other pieces, for using just enough force to reach the goal.

[My Chess Career, Conclusion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Conclusion) (first published in 1920; doc_58ad5fec36d64d41)

- "You must also have the courage of your convictions. If you think your move is good, make it." (My Chess Career, Conclusion; first published in 1920)
- "When attacking other pieces use just sufficient force to attain your object." (My Chess Career, Conclusion; first published in 1920)

Limits: Advice from a lecture to club players; conviction does not replace calculation.

## K17-career-up-to-1920

According to My Chess Career, Capablanca was born in Havana on 19 November 1888 and learned the game before he was five, watching his father play. At twelve he beat J. Corzo in a match and became, in his words, the moral champion of Cuba; for that he first studied books on endings.

[My Chess Career, chapter 2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_2) (first published in 1920; doc_4f35d90d063648be); [My Chess Career, chapter 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/My_Chess_Career/Chapter_1) (first published in 1920; doc_fac91271a74346df)

- "I was born in Habana, the capital of the Island of Cuba, the 19th of November, 1888." (My Chess Career, chapter 1; first published in 1920)
- "I was not yet five years old when by accident I came into my father's private office and found him playing with another gentleman." (My Chess Career, chapter 1; first published in 1920)
- "The victory made me, morally at least, the champion of Cuba. I was then twelve years old." (My Chess Career, chapter 2; first published in 1920)
- "One of them gave me several books, among which, one on endings. I liked the endings and studied some of them." (My Chess Career, chapter 2; first published in 1920)

Limits: An autobiographical account from 1920. Talk about him in the third person ("Capablanca tells that...") and never as your own memory.
